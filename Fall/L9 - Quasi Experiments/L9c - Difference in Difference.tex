\begin{frame}{How do we construct the counterfactual?}
We observe $Y_{it}(D_{it})$ but never $Y_{it}(1-D_{it})$. Four ways to fill in the missing cell:
\begin{description}
	\item [Before--after:] use the treated unit's own past, $Y_{i,t-1}(0)$. Fails if anything else changed over time.
	\item [Matching:] use a different unit $j$ with $X_j = X_i$ at the same date. Fails if they differ in
	unobservables.
	\item [\alert{Difference-in-differences:}] use the \emph{change} in other units,
	$Y_{jt}(0) - Y_{j,t-1}(0)$, as the counterfactual change for $i$. Levels can differ; trends cannot.
	\item [Synthetic control:] use a weighted average of other units chosen to track $i$ before
	treatment (Session 11).
\end{description}
\end{frame}


\section{The two-by-two}

\begin{frame}{Setup}
\begin{itemize}
	\item Two groups, $D_i \in \{0, 1\}$; two periods, $t \in \{1, 2\}$. The treated group is treated in period 2 only:
	$D_{it} = D_i \cdot Post_t$.
\end{itemize}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & Treated group ($D_i = 1$) & Control group ($D_i = 0$) \\
\midrule
Period 1 (before) & $Y_{i1}(0)$ & $Y_{i1}(0)$ \\
Period 2 (after) & $Y_{i2}(1)$ & $Y_{i2}(0)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
	\item The target is the effect on the treated in period 2,
	\begin{align*}
		\text{ATT} = \E\left[\,Y_{i2}(1) - Y_{i2}(0) \mid D_i = 1\,\right].
	\end{align*}
	\item The missing cell is $\E[Y_{i2}(0) \mid D_i = 1]$: what the treated group would have done
	without treatment.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Two differences}
\small
Write the change in the outcome as $\Delta Y_i = Y_{i2} - Y_{i1}$. For the treated group:
\begin{align*}
	\E[\Delta Y_i \mid D_i=1] = \overbrace{\E\left[Y_{i2}(1) - Y_{i2}(0) \mid D_i=1 \right]}^{\alert{\text{ATT}}}
	+ \overbrace{\E\left[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i=1 \right]}^{\alert{\text{trend}(1)}}
\end{align*}
For the control group:
\begin{align*}
	\E[\Delta Y_i \mid D_i=0] = \overbrace{\E\left[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i=0 \right]}^{\alert{\text{trend}(0)}}
\end{align*}
The difference in differences:
\begin{align*}
	\Delta_{DD} = \E[\Delta Y_i \mid D_i=1] - \E[\Delta Y_i \mid D_i=0]
	= \text{ATT} + \big[\text{trend}(1) - \text{trend}(0)\big].
\end{align*}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Parallel trends}
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\Delta_{DD} = \text{ATT}$ exactly when the bracket is zero:
	\begin{align*}
		\E\left[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i=1 \right] = \E\left[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i=0 \right].
	\end{align*}
	Absent treatment, both groups would have \alert{changed} by the same amount on average.
	\medskip
	\item What it allows:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Different \alert{levels}: $\E[Y_{it}(0) \mid D_i = 1] \neq \E[Y_{it}(0) \mid D_i = 0]$. Selection on
		anything permanent is fine.
		\item Selection on \alert{gains}: the treated may benefit more than the controls would. That is why the
		target is the ATT, not the ATE.
	\end{itemize}
	\medskip
	\item What it rules out: anything that moves the two groups' untreated outcomes \emph{differently} between
	the periods.
	\medskip
	\item It is an assumption about a counterfactual. No data on the post period can test it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Running example: an internal-control mandate}

\begin{frame}{Running example: SOX Section 404}
\begin{itemize}
	\item The Sarbanes--Oxley Act (July 2002), Section 404: management must report on internal
	controls and the auditor must attest to that report.
	\medskip
	\item \alert{Accelerated filers} (public float $\ge$ \$75M) first complied for fiscal years ending on or
	after November 15, 2004. Smaller firms were exempt for years.
	\medskip
	\item Question: what did compliance do to audit fees? (For real firms near the threshold, Iliev 2010
	estimates the management report raised audit fees by 98\%.)
	\medskip
	\item \alert{Our simulation:} 2,000 firms, fiscal 2000--2007, log audit fees.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Firm effects (big firms pay more) and common year shocks.
		\item True effect on accelerated filers: $+0.35$ in 2004, decaying to $+0.20$ by 2007 as auditors learn.
		\item One complication, on purpose: larger firms are on a steeper audit-fee trend.
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Four ways to compute the same number}
\small
Take fiscal 2003 (before) and 2004 (after). $D_i = 1$ for accelerated filers.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \alert{Cell means}: $(\bar y_{T,04} - \bar y_{T,03}) - (\bar y_{C,04} - \bar y_{C,03})$.
	\item \alert{First differences}: regress $\Delta y_i = y_{i,04} - y_{i,03}$ on a constant and $D_i$:
	$\quad \Delta y_i = \beta_{post} + \delta \cdot D_i + \Delta\varepsilon_i$.
	\item \alert{Interaction regression} on the stacked panel:
	$\quad y_{it} = \beta_0 + \alpha \cdot D_i + \gamma \cdot Post_t + \delta \cdot D_i \times Post_t + \varepsilon_{it}$.
	\item \alert{Two-way fixed effects}:
	$\quad y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \delta \cdot D_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}$.
\end{enumerate}
\medskip
In (3), $\alpha$ is the level gap in 2003, $\gamma$ the control group's change, $\delta$ the DiD.
\medskip

\end{center}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why four routes?}
\begin{itemize}
	\item With two periods and a balanced panel they are \alert{numerically identical}: same point
	estimate, and the same standard error once you cluster by firm in (3) and (4).
	\medskip
	\item Each one is useful for something:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item (1) is what you put in a table and explain to a manager.
		\item (2) makes the identifying assumption visible: $\E[\Delta\varepsilon_i \mid D_i] = 0$ \emph{is}
		parallel trends.
		\item (3) gives you the level gap $\alpha$, which is \emph{allowed} to be large.
		\item (4) extends to many periods, many units and covariates. It is also the version that breaks
		with staggered timing (Lecture 9b).
	\end{itemize}
	\medskip
	\item But our estimate is $0.436$ and the truth is $0.35$. The arithmetic was fine. The assumption was not.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Every comparison, against the truth}
\begin{center}

\end{center}
\medskip
\begin{itemize}
	\item \alert{Post-period gap} confounds the effect with the permanent level gap: big firms always paid more.
	\item \alert{Before--after} confounds it with whatever happened to all audit fees that year.
	\item \alert{DiD} removes both, but not a \emph{difference} in trends. Accelerated filers are big, and

\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Conditional parallel trends}

\begin{frame}{Conditional parallel trends}
\begin{itemize}
	\item Parallel trends may fail unconditionally but hold \emph{within} groups of similar firms:
	\begin{align*}
		\E\left[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid X_i, D_i = 1\right] = \E\left[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid X_i, D_i = 0\right].
	\end{align*}
	\item It is the matching assumption applied to the \emph{change} in the outcome.
	\medskip
	\item Simplest implementation: let the year effects depend on pre-treatment covariates,
	\begin{align*}
		y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \textstyle\sum_s \beta_s \cdot \mathbb{1}\{t = s\} \cdot X_i + \delta \cdot D_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}.
	\end{align*}
	\item With two periods this is the first-difference regression with $X_i$ added: $\Delta y_i$ on $D_i$ and $X_i$.
	\medskip
	\item In the SOX example, $X_i$ = firm size. Size $\times$ year moves the estimate from 0.436 to 0.328
	(truth 0.35).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

                  sox, vcov = ~firm)                                                  # + size x year

\end{lstlisting}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]

                    data=sox, vcov={"CRV1": "firm"})

\end{lstlisting}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \texttt{i(year, size)} interacts each year dummy with a \emph{continuous} variable: one size slope per year.
	\item Use covariates measured \emph{before} treatment. Here size is fixed at its pre-SOX value.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Conditioning needs overlap}
\begin{itemize}
	\item Why not size-decile $\times$ year fixed effects? Because treatment is assigned \emph{by} size.
	Every decile except the one containing \$75M is all treated or all exempt.
	\medskip
	\item Within-cell comparisons then exist only for firms near the cutoff. That is a regression
	discontinuity (Session 10), and it is what Iliev (2010) estimates.
	\medskip
	\item The linear size $\times$ year model works here because the simulated size trend really is linear.
	It extrapolates the small firms' size--trend relationship to the big ones.
	\medskip
	\item General lesson: conditional parallel trends needs treated and control units at the \alert{same}
	values of $X$. When it fails, you are relying on the functional form.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Covariates done right}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \alert{Time-invariant, pre-treatment $X_i$} interacted with time: what we just did. Safe.
	\medskip
	\item \alert{Time-varying $X_{it}$ that treatment can move} is a bad control. SOX changes internal-control
	spending, audit hours and auditor choice. Controlling for any of them blocks part of the effect (mediator)
	or opens a collider path (Session 5).
	\medskip
	\item Rule of thumb: condition on covariates measured \emph{before} treatment, or on ones the treatment
	cannot plausibly affect.
	\medskip
	\item \alert{Doubly robust DiD} (Sant'Anna and Zhao 2020): combine an outcome regression for $\Delta Y$
	among controls with a propensity score for $D_i$ given $X_i$. It is consistent if either one is right.
	That is AIPW (Session 11) applied to the change in the outcome.
	In \texttt{R}: \texttt{DRDID}, or \texttt{did::att\_gt(..., est\_method = "dr")}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{What parallel trends is and isn't}

\begin{frame}{Parallel trends depends on the scale}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{fig_did_logs.pdf}
\end{center}
\vspace{-0.3cm}
\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}
	\item Both store types grow 4\% a period and the promotion does nothing. Trends are parallel in logs, so they
	cannot be parallel in levels.
	\item It can hold in every monotone transformation only if it holds for the whole distribution of $Y(0)$
	(Roth and Sant'Anna 2023). Decide on substance whether the counterfactual change is additive or proportional.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Flat pre-trends are not parallel trends}
\begin{itemize}
	\item From Session 8: a pre-period that looks flat is reassuring, not proof.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Pre-tests have low power against the violations that matter (Roth 2022).
		\item Report what the pre-period rules out, and how the estimate moves if post-period violations are
		as large as pre-period ones (Rambachan and Roth 2023; \texttt{HonestDiD}).
	\end{itemize}
	\medskip
	\item \alert{Compound treatments}: parallel trends is about what would have happened \emph{at the date of
	treatment}. Anything else that changed in 2004 for large firms but not small ones is in the estimate.
	Examples: other rules phased in by size, exchange listing standards, a change in how large audit firms priced.
	\medskip
	\item The question to ask: \emph{why} did the treated group get treated \emph{then}? If the answer involves
	the outcome, worry.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\section{Threats to the design}

\begin{frame}{The Ashenfelter dip (Heckman and Smith 1999)}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[height=0.7\textheight]{./resources/ashenfelter_dip.png}
\end{center}
{\small Training participants' earnings fall just before they enrol. They enrol \emph{because} of a bad draw.}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Ashenfelter dip: algebra}
\small
\begin{itemize}
	\item Model $Y_{it}(0) = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + u_{it}$, with $u_{it}$ i.i.d.\ over time. Units enter treatment in period 2
	if $Y_{i1} < B$, so $u_{i1} < B - \alpha_i - \gamma_1$.
	\medskip
	\item The permanent part $\alpha_i$ differences out. The \emph{transitory} part does not:
	\begin{align*}
		\E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 1] &= \gamma_2 - \gamma_1 - \E[u_{i1} \mid u_{i1} < B - \alpha_i - \gamma_1], \\
		\E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 0] &= \gamma_2 - \gamma_1 - \E[u_{i1} \mid u_{i1} \ge B - \alpha_i - \gamma_1].
	\end{align*}
	\item The difference is $\E[u_{i1} \mid u_{i1} \ge c_i] - \E[u_{i1} \mid u_{i1} < c_i] > 0$, writing
	$c_i = B - \alpha_i - \gamma_1$. DiD overstates the effect.
	\medskip
	\item Regression to the mean: the unlucky recover whether or not they are treated.
	\medskip
	\item Business versions: firms hire a consultant, replace a CEO, or adopt a new system \emph{after} a bad quarter.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{More threats, in SOX terms}
\small
\begin{itemize}
	\item \alert{Composition.} Treatment changes who is in each group.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Some firms went private or deregistered to escape SOX costs (Engel, Hayes and Wang 2007;
		Leuz, Triantis and Wang 2008).
		\item Some kept their public float below \$75M to stay exempt (Gao, Wu and Zimmerman 2009).
		\item Fix: define groups by pre-period characteristics (float in 2002), keep a balanced panel, and report
		attrition by group.
	\end{itemize}
	\item \alert{Anticipation.} The Act passed in 2002 and the rules were set in 2003--04. Firms that start paying
	for compliance in 2003 contaminate the base year. Use an earlier base period.
	\item \alert{Spillovers onto controls.} If auditors are capacity-constrained, compliance work for big clients
	raises fees for small clients too. The control group is then partly treated, and DiD understates the effect
	(the interference problem from Session 5).
	\item \alert{Selection on the outcome.} The Ashenfelter dip, again: here the timing is set by law, which is
	exactly why it is a good design.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\section{Inference}

\begin{frame}{Serial correlation: placebo laws (Bertrand, Duflo and Mullainathan 2004)}
\begin{itemize}
	\item A state-by-year panel of log e-commerce sales, 20 years, AR(1) errors. Half the states ``pass a law'' in a
	random year. The law does nothing. Rejection rates of a nominal 5\% test, 1,000 simulations:
\end{itemize}
\begin{center}
\small
\input{tab_did_bdm.tex}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
	\item With persistent errors, the treatment dummy is persistent too. Conventional SEs treat 20 correlated
	years as 20 independent draws.
	\item \alert{Cluster at the level at which treatment is assigned} (the state), so each state's whole time
	series counts as one draw. That needs enough clusters, and enough \emph{treated} clusters.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Inference with few treated clusters}
\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Card and Krueger's SEs treat 410 stores as independent, but the policy varies by \emph{state}: NJ vs.\ PA.
\item Stores in a state share shocks (local demand, weather). Cluster-robust SEs need many clusters, and
many \emph{treated} clusters. With $G = 2$ you cannot cluster by state at all.
\item The wild cluster bootstrap (Cameron, Gelbach and Miller 2008) fixes \emph{few clusters}, not \emph{few treated
clusters}. With one or two treated clusters it still over-rejects (MacKinnon and Webb 2018).
\item With one treated group and \emph{many} control groups:
\begin{itemize}
\item Conley and Taber (2011): the control groups' residuals estimate the distribution of the treated group's error.
\item Randomization inference: assign the policy to each control group in turn (the placebo logic of Session 11).
\item Aggregate to group $\times$ period and use $G-1$ degrees of freedom (Donald and Lang 2007).
\end{itemize}
\item With one control group, as in Card and Krueger, none of these can deliver policy-level inference. Count your
treated (and control) clusters before you report a standard error.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\section{Variants and applications}

\begin{frame}{Triple differences}
\begin{itemize}
	\item Example: a state privacy law restricts ad targeting. Compare in-state vs.\ out-of-state ($S$),
	before vs.\ after ($P$), and ad-supported vs.\ subscription apps ($G$). Subscription apps should be unaffected.
	\begin{align*}
		y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 S + \beta_2 G + \beta_3 P + \beta_4 SG + \beta_5 SP + \beta_6 GP + \alert{\delta}\, SGP + \varepsilon
	\end{align*}
	\item $\hat\delta$ is the DiD for ad-supported apps minus the DiD for subscription apps. Include \emph{every}
	two-way interaction (or the corresponding fixed effects).
	\medskip
	\item Identification needs only one parallel-trends assumption: the \emph{gap} between the two app types'
	trends would have been the same in and out of state. Neither DiD needs to be unbiased on its own
	(Olden and M{\o}en 2022).
	\medskip
	\item It is weaker than parallel trends, not free: a shock that hits only ad-supported apps in the law
	state still biases it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{DiD in your fields}
\small
\begin{itemize}
	\item \alert{Accounting.}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Mandatory IFRS adoption around the world and its liquidity and cost-of-capital effects
		(Daske, Hail, Leuz and Verdi 2008, \emph{JAR}).
		\item SOX 404 costs and earnings quality at the \$75M threshold (Iliev 2010, \emph{JF}).
		\item Firms managing their float to stay exempt (Gao, Wu and Zimmerman 2009, \emph{JAR}): the composition
		threat in action.
	\end{itemize}
	\item \alert{Marketing / IS.}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Book reviews on two retailers' sites: the change in a title's relative sales rank against the change in
		its relative reviews (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, \emph{JMR}).
		\item A city soda tax: prices, cross-border shopping and purchases relative to untaxed comparison areas
		(Seiler, Tuchman and Yao 2021, \emph{JMR}).
	\end{itemize}
	\item Reading one: find the $2\times2$, name the control group, and ask what else changed for the treated group
	at that date.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Toward Lecture 9b: SOX was phased in}
\begin{itemize}
	\item Accelerated filers: full Section 404 from fiscal years ending on or after November 15, 2004.
	\item Smaller firms: the management report from fiscal years ending on or after December 15, 2007.
	\item The auditor attestation for small firms never arrived. Dodd--Frank (2010) exempted them.
	\medskip
	\item That is \alert{staggered adoption}: different cohorts treated at different dates. The natural regression
	is the same two-way FE model with $D_{it}$ switching on at each cohort's date.
	\medskip
	\item It now makes comparisons we never asked for, including \emph{already-treated} firms serving as controls.
	When effects evolve over time, as they do here (0.35 decaying to 0.20), those comparisons are biased.
	\medskip
	\item Lecture 9b: what that regression estimates, and the estimators that fix it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Recap}
\begin{itemize}
	\item DiD replaces ``same level'' with ``same \emph{change}''. Permanent differences are fine; differential
	changes are not.
	\medskip
	\item In a two-period panel, the four routes (means, first differences, interaction, two-way FE) give the
	same number. That number is right only if parallel trends holds.
	\medskip
	\item Parallel trends is scale-dependent and untestable. Pre-periods are diagnostics. Conditioning on
	pre-treatment $X$ helps when there is overlap.
	\medskip
	\item Threats: selection on transitory shocks, composition, anticipation, spillovers, compound treatments.
	\medskip
	\item Inference: cluster at the level of assignment, and count the treated clusters.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
